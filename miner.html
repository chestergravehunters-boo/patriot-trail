⭐ Dr. John Miner

(1760–1804, age 44)

Private, 6th & 7th Connecticut Regiments
Brother of: Justice Miner, Founding Father of Chesterland

Born: Norfolk, Litchfield County, Connecticut • July 7, 1760
Enlisted: Norfolk, CT (1776) at age 16
Service: 6th Connecticut Regiment • 7th Connecticut Regiment (Short Levies)
Battles/Actions: New York campaign (1776) • Short term service in 1780
Discharged: After short levy service, 1780
Burial: Old Settlement Cemetery, Chester Township, Ohio (unmarked; row ten)

⭐ Dr. John Miner — Revolutionary War Service

Dr. John Miner was born in Norfolk, Connecticut, on July 7, 1760, the son of Dr. John Miner and Sarah (Dutton) Miner — and the older brother of Justice Miner, who would later become the Founding Father of Chesterland.

He enlisted on July 1, 1776, and arrived with the 6th Connecticut Regiment in New York on July 15, serving two months and twenty‑seven days during the early New York campaign under Col. John Chester and Capt. Chester Wells.

His name later appears on the payroll of the Short Levies of 1780 in Capt. Converse’s company of the 7th Connecticut Regiment, commanded by Col. Heman Swift. These brief enlistments were typical of Connecticut militia service during the war’s most demanding years.

Although his wartime role was as a private, he later became known locally as “Dr. Miner,” a title he carried into his years as an early settler in the Western Reserve.

⭐ Life on the Early American Frontier

After the Revolution, Dr. Miner married Loraine (Rice) Royce in Norfolk, Connecticut, in 1782. They raised their children together through years of steady westward movement, first to Pennsylvania and later to Harpersfield in the Western Reserve. There, on November 21, 1801, Loraine died.

Seeking a new beginning for his children, Dr. Miner came to Chester Township in 1803, following the path already taken by his brother Justice Miner. He took up a lot near the Crossroads, cleared a small piece of land, and sowed wheat. He built the cabin that was meant to shelter his children and give them a fresh start on the frontier.

⭐ The Wind‑Fall of 1804 — A Pioneer Tragedy

On July 22, 1804, a violent wind fall — what we would now call a tornado — tore through Chester Township, cutting a swath through the timber like a scythe. Only the day before, Dr. Miner had moved his children into the cabin he had finished enough to live in, though the floor was still dirt and the chimney had not yet been built.

It was the Sabbath, and he had lain down in the cabin to rest while the children played outside. They were the first to hear the rising roar in the woods and ran to rouse him.

Dr. Miner told them to get down at once beneath the sleepers, into the deep hollow where the chimney foundation would be. With no chimney yet raised, that space beneath the heavy base logs was the safest place he could put them.

He opened the door to see what the wind had wrought and, as he stepped back inside, three great trees came upon the house and crushed the roof, knocking the timber above the doorway down upon him. He was killed instantly — but his children, sheltered exactly where he had placed them, survived without harm.

His daughter Polly, just thirteen, went at once to her father, but nothing could be done. Fearing her little sister and brother might wander or come to harm while she sought help, she set them side by side in chairs and secured them there so they would remain safe.

She then set out alone, running a mile through the destruction to the home of her uncle Justice Miner for aid — an act of courage far beyond her years, and one that reflects the resolve that built this community.

Dr. Miner was first buried near the Crossroads, but when the Chillicothe Road was laid out in 1818–19, his remains were moved to the Old Settlement Cemetery. His grave is today unmarked, but is believed to lie in row ten, near the stone of his brother Justice. The Chester Grave Hunters are currently working to secure a government‑issued marker to formally recognize his Revolutionary War service.

⭐ Legacy in Chesterland

Dr. John Miner stands among the earliest settlers of Chester Township — a Revolutionary War veteran whose life bridged Connecticut’s wartime service and the founding of new communities in the Western Reserve. As the brother of Justice Miner, his story is inseparable from the earliest chapter of Chesterland’s history.

Today, he is honored on the Patriot Trail as one of the township’s foundational figures, remembered for both his service in the Revolution and his role in the first years of settlement.
