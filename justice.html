⭐ Justice Miner — Revolutionary War Service

Justice Miner was born in 1762 in the Norfolk–Woodbury region of Litchfield County, Connecticut, the son of Dr. John Miner and Sarah (Dutton) Miner — and the younger brother of Dr. John Miner, also a Revolutionary War soldier.

According to the official West Point return, Justice Miner mustered at Norfolk, Connecticut, and served as a private in Col. Samuel Canfield’s Connecticut Militia Regiment. The return, certified at West Point on September 15, 1781, was prepared under direct orders from General George Washington and the Connecticut Council of Safety.

This single surviving record places Justice Miner at West Point during the critical weeks leading up to Yorktown, when Washington executed his bold deception campaign — pretending to prepare for an attack on the British who held New York while secretly marching the Continental Army south to trap Cornwallis at Yorktown.

Canfield’s regiment was part of the force Washington relied on to hold the Hudson Highlands and maintain the illusion of a northern attack. Justice Miner was discharged in 1783, when the war formally ended.

⭐ A Pioneer of the Western Reserve

Justice married Mabel Plumb of Norfolk in 1779. By 1801, he and several family members were living in Charleston, Genesee County, New York, when they resolved to move west into the newly opened Western Reserve. In April of that year, Justice, his son Philo, his son‑in‑law Harvey Sheffield, and two others drove forty head of cattle across the frontier to the lands they had purchased in what would become Chester Township.

On May 1, 1801, Justice, Philo, and Sheffield made the first clearing and built the first log house in the township. After returning briefly to New York, the full party began the permanent move in February 1802, reaching Burton by mid‑May. By July 1802, Justice Miner had brought his family to their new home in the settlement then called Wooster, later Chester.

Justice Miner quickly became one of the township’s most respected leaders. At the first town meeting after incorporation on November 16, 1816, he served as chairman and was chosen as one of the first trustees. He later served five years as justice of the peace, giving his name to the office he held.

His first wife, Mabel, died in 1811. Justice married Hannah Morse (Moss) on January 1, 1812. She died in 1831.

⭐ Family and Later Years

The Miners raised a large family whose names appear throughout early Chester records: son Philo, daughter Charlotte Sheffield, twin girls Sarah & Anna, John, Betsy, Matilda, and son Origen. By the time he wrote his will in 1850, he had outlived many of his children, while others had moved farther west.

Justice Miner died on July 27, 1850, aged 88 years and 6 months. His stone in the Old Settlement Cemetery does not mention his military service, though he was recorded in 1925 as a “Sol. of Amer. Rev.” The Chester Grave Hunters are currently working to secure a government‑issued marker to formally recognize his Revolutionary War service.

⭐ Legacy in Chesterland

Justice Miner stands as one of the Founding Fathers of Chesterland — a Revolutionary War veteran who carved the first clearing, built the first cabin, chaired the first town meeting, and helped establish the civic foundations of the community.

His presence at West Point in 1781 ties Chesterland directly to the decisive final chapter of the American Revolution. Today, he is honored on the Patriot Trail as a man whose life bridged the Revolution and the settlement of the Western Reserve, and whose legacy remains visible in the very ground he helped clear.
