⭐ James Gillmore

(1754–1829, age 76)

Private, Massachusetts Militia • Early Settler of Chester Township

Born: Pelham, New Hampshire • November 14, 1752
Enlisted: Hampshire County, Massachusetts (1777)
Service: Lt. Josiah Wilson’s Company, Col. Porter’s Hampshire County Regiment
Battles/Actions: Northern alarm to reinforce Maj. Gen. Gates (1777)
Discharged: October 17, 1777
Burial: Old Settlement Cemetery, Chester Township, Ohio • Row 10

⭐ Revolutionary War Service

James Gillmore was born in Pelham, New Hampshire, in 1754, the son of James and Mary (Crow) Gillmore. By the late 1760s he was living in Murrayfield (now Chester), Massachusetts, where his name appears on the 1768 assessment roll as an early settler.

During the Revolution, he served briefly in the Hampshire County militia. On September 23, 1777, he enlisted as a private in Lt. Josiah Wilson’s company, part of Col. Porter’s Hampshire County Regiment. His company marched north on an emergency alarm to reinforce the army under Maj. Gen. Horatio Gates during the Saratoga campaign. He was discharged October 17, 1777, having served one month and twenty‑three days, including travel.

Though short, this type of rapid‑response militia service was typical of Massachusetts men during the crisis moments of the war.

⭐ Life in Massachusetts and the Move West

James married a woman named Nancy, though her maiden name and marriage date are not recorded. By the time he left Massachusetts, he had six children and had lived in both Blandford and Chester, Massachusetts.

In 1810, James and his three sons — James Jr., Edmund, and William — made plans to migrate west into the Connecticut Western Reserve. They exchanged their Massachusetts lands for timbered lands in Ohio, spending a full year preparing clothing, tools, and supplies for the journey. Neighbors saved apple seeds for them all winter so the family could plant orchards in their new home.

⭐ The 1811 Migration

In the spring of 1811, the three Gillmores set out in a remarkable caravan: two large wagons per family, each covered with tow‑cloth stretched over curved hoops; two yoke of oxen pulling each wagon; and a cow hitched to the tail of every wagon. The journey lasted all summer, with the last fifty miles requiring them to cut their own road through the wilderness. It was one of the great pioneer migrations into what would become Chester Township, Ohio.

⭐ Arrival in Chester Township

In 1812, James Gillmore Sr. arrived with five of his children and built a log house half a mile east of the township center. He later built the first frame house in Chester Township, near the present‑day town hall.

He quickly became part of the civic life of the new settlement. He was likely the township treasurer in 1818 and 1820, though the records do not distinguish between father and son. In 1828, James Jr. served on a committee to secure land for a burying ground. When the township could not legally purchase the land, James Gillmore Sr. offered an acre north of the center for $15 — the land that became the Chester Township Cemetery.

⭐ Death and Burial

James Gillmore died in Chester Township on January 30, 1829, aged 76. He is buried in the Old Settlement Cemetery in Chesterland, in Row 10, among the earliest pioneers of the township.

⭐ Legacy in Chesterland

James Gillmore’s life traces the arc of early American settlement: born in New Hampshire, a young settler in western Massachusetts, a militia private during the Revolution, and a pioneer who crossed hundreds of miles by ox‑drawn wagon to the Western Reserve. He helped build Chester Township’s earliest homes and civic institutions.

His family’s 1811–1812 migration stands as one of the most vivid pioneer stories in Chesterland’s history. Today, he is honored on the Patriot Trail as a Revolutionary War veteran and a founding settler whose family helped shape the community that followed.
